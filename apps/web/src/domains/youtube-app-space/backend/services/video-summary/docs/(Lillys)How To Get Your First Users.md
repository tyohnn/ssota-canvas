

## 📌 How do you get your first users for a new product?
 Find early adopters and those with a burning need by <mark>charging real money early, using targeted personal outreach, launching fast, studying them closely, and experimenting rapidly</mark>. 


## 💡 What kind of product should you build initially?
 A <mark>minimum evolvable product</mark> that can survive contact with early users and adapt fast based on their feedback. 


This content reveals the counterintuitive strategy for acquiring your **first users**, shifting the focus from persuasion to a **search problem**. Learn to identify "Gustavs and Ankas"—early adopters with a **burning issue**—who will provide sharper feedback than free users. Discover why you need a **minimum evolvable product** and how early user feedback path-dependently steers your product's ultimate evolution, using Tesla as a compelling case study.
## 1. Finding Your First Users: A Search Problem 
![image](https://resource-release.s3.ap-northeast-2.amazonaws.com/thumbnails/0kARDVL2nZg/0.jpg)
1. **The Challenge of Early Adoption** 
  1. Most people are not early adopters of new products. 
  2. Few people have been among the first 10 users of any product they use. 
  3. Almost no one wants to be a startup's first paying customer. 
  4. Yet, great products still find people willing to take the leap. 

2. **The Minimum Evolvable Product (MEP)** 
  1. The earliest product version only needs to survive contact with a tiny group. 
  2. You are not building the final form of the product. 
  3. You are building something that has the ability to evolve. 
  4. You need a **minimum evolvable product** (MEP), not just a minimum viable product. 

3. **Identifying Early Adopters (Gustavs and Ankas)** 
  1. Some people enjoy being early adopters, like a colleague named Gustav. 
  2. Others have a **burning issue** and will try any new product that might help. 
  3. <em>Example</em>: The speaker's team needed an inference API quickly. 
  1. They paid a startup whose product solved the issue within 3 days. 
  2. The speaker was the startup's first customer. 
  3. The startup's size or reputation did not matter; the problem did. 
  4. **Finding first users is a search problem**, not a persuasion problem. 
  5. You are looking for the **Gustavs and Ankas** of the world. 
  6. These are people who try new things or have a burning need you can solve. 


## 2. Counterintuitive Implications for Early User Acquisition 
![image](https://resource-release.s3.ap-northeast-2.amazonaws.com/thumbnails/0kARDVL2nZg/92.jpg)
1. **Charge Real Money Early** 
  1. Early adopters and those with a burning problem are rarely price sensitive. 
  2. The goal is **feedback**, not revenue. 
  3. Paying customers provide **sharper feedback** than free users. 
  4. An angry paying customer is more likely to give feedback than a free user. 

2. **Targeted Outreach and Launch Strategy** 
  1. Use **targeted personal outreach** to find these people. 
  2. A billboard is less effective than a targeted cold email or a knock on the door. 
  3. **Launch early**, as YC has always preached. 
  4. You need a wide surface area for early users to find you. 

3. **Studying and Experimenting with Early Users** 
  1. Study your early users closely, like an anthropologist. 
  2. Understand how they make decisions and why they chose to trust you. 
  3. **Experiment fast** and do not fear churn. 
  4. Run constant experiments on pricing, landing pages, and features. 
  5. Try to make early users love the product. 
  6. Do not stress if you lose one of them. 
  7. Annoyed customers can often be fixed because the relationship is personal. 
  8. Churn is acceptable because many others have not heard of you yet. 
  9. Startups have an advantage: bad experiments do not generate headlines. 
  10. You are fighting relevance, not headlines. 

4. **The Business Model Implication** 
  1. Early users may shape who you are building for initially. 
  2. Most people do not pay for many consumer apps. 
  3. The average personal software spend is tiny (e.g., $150/month total). 
  4. Corporate cards often have tools costing more than that amount individually. 
  5. This gap matters in the AI era. 
  6. Consumer apps struggle because ads may not cover AI costs. 
  7. Subscriptions must fit into an already small personal budget. 
  8. Many AI founders start by selling to **prosumers** or **businesses**. 
  9. They also target users like doctors who have high advertising value. 


## 3. Path-Dependent Product Evolution: The Tesla Case Study 
![image](https://resource-release.s3.ap-northeast-2.amazonaws.com/thumbnails/0kARDVL2nZg/202.jpg)
1. **Early Users Steer Product Evolution** 
  1. Early users do more than just give feedback. 
  2. They steer how your product evolves over time. 
  3. An analogy helps founders think about their first users. 

2. **The Phylogenetic Tree Analogy** 
  1. Think of a startup as a **phylogenetic tree**. 
  2. The root node is an <em>amoeba</em>; leaf nodes are complex organisms like humans. 
  3. Most mature products have evolved from an amoeba to a human or dog. 
  4. Mature products have millions of users, a refined sales pitch, and clear value. 
  5. Early startups are like amoebas with basic functions exposed to external pressures. 
  6. Founders run an evolutionary search through potential future directions. 

3. **Tesla Roadster: The Amoeba Stage** 
  1. Consider **Tesla** as a case study. 
  2. Their amoeba was the **Tesla Roadster**. 
  3. <em>Lore</em>: Tesla needed a high-margin product to fund capex for Model S and Model 3/Y. 
  4. <em>Second Interpretation</em>: Tesla was searching for early adopters. 
  5. They sought people willing to buy an impractical $150,000 car. 
  6. This car had limited range, little storage, no public charging, and looked strange. 

4. **Path-Dependence in Tesla's Evolution** 
  1. Product evolution is **path-dependent** on what early adopters wanted. 
  2. <em>Example</em>: The mass-market Tesla Model Y has fast acceleration and better tech than competitors. 
  3. However, it has worse suspension and comfort than a Toyota. 
  4. Early adopters cared more about tech and acceleration than comfort. 
  5. A mass-market vehicle designed in a vacuum likely would not have sub-3 second zero to 60. 
  6. This outcome resulted from the search algorithm Tesla ran. 
  7. If early adopters wanted a slow, plush vehicle, Tesla's cars would look different today. 

5. **The Minimum Evolvable Product Conclusion** 
  1. This is the algorithm YC helps founders run. 
  2. Your first version must be a **minimum evolvable product** (MEP). 
  3. The MEP is simple and can respond to market pressures. 
  4. It must survive contact with early users and adapt fast based on their push. 
  5. Knowing the product will change is freeing; it does not need to be perfect initially. 
  6. The ultimate product depends on where you begin and who you begin it with.
